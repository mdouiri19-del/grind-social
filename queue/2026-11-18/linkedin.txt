Doing 10 problems from the same chapter in a row feels like progress. Then the exam mixes every chapter together.

When all your practice questions come from one topic, you know the method before you read the question. Question 3 is a chain rule question because questions 1 and 2 were.

The exam takes that clue away. Now the hard part is knowing which method each question needs.

Learning researchers call the fix interleaving. You mix problem types in one session instead of doing them in blocks.

It feels worse while you do it. You get more wrong and you go slower. But in studies on math practice, students who mixed did better on the test that came later, because they had practised the choosing step.

A way to try it this week:

Take 3 topics from your next exam and write 3 problems from each. Shuffle all 9. Before you solve one, write down the method you think it needs.

Every time you pick the wrong method, you've found the topic to review first.
